# Chapter 01 — Presentation Is Everything

Presentation is everything.

That is why I wore white to meet a woman who believed she was making a deal with the Devil.

At 2:13 on Saturday morning, rain had turned Mercy Avenue into a river of reflected neon. In the city of Vesper, the Halo Café was the only place where the coffee remained hot, the security cameras remained broken, and the owner knew better than to remember faces.

A train passed overhead every six minutes. The windows trembled, the lights flickered, and for nine glorious seconds no microphone in the world could hear what was being said.

Privacy is rarely a place. Usually, it is timing.

The woman waiting in my booth looked about twenty-five and exhausted enough to be fifty. Wet strands of black hair clung to her cheeks. A hospital bracelet protruded from beneath one sleeve, and she kept checking the door as though something might follow her through it.

Her phone lay on the table between us.

A white flame burned against its black screen.

**YOUR LIGHT HAS ARRIVED.**

That was me.

Tasteless? Perhaps.

Memorable? Absolutely.

“Are you Lucifer?” she asked.

“Tonight.”

Her fingers tightened around her coffee cup. She had not taken a single sip.

“Who are you really?”

Probably the same question you are asking.

We can skip that part. Names are useful when you need someone to blame. What matters is what I can do for you.

“My clients call me Lucifer,” I said. “You may do the same.”

“That isn’t a real name.”

“I chose Lucifer. The city chose Vesper. Half the passports here still carry its old name. Yet here we are.”

She did not smile. Tough audience.

I glanced at her phone. Her request occupied the center of the screen:

**FIND ELENA VALE. BRING HER HOME BEFORE SUNRISE.**

Sunrise was due at 5:38.

The request gave me three hours and twenty-five minutes.

Beneath the request, two fields remained empty.

**PRICE: PENDING**

**AGREEMENT: PENDING**

“Your sister?” I asked.

Her eyes narrowed. “How do you know that?”

“The app asked for access to your contacts, photographs, messages, location history, and medical records. You pressed *Allow All*.”

“I didn’t give it access to my medical records.”

“No,” I said. “You gave it access to everything.”

She looked down at the hospital bracelet and hurriedly pulled her sleeve over it.

Humans are adorable when they discover that convenience has teeth.

“What exactly is a Light?” she asked.

“A Light is what you need when every ordinary door has stopped opening. A name. A key. Safe passage. Medicine after every pharmacy has locked its doors, when waiting for morning might cost a life. An introduction to someone who does not officially exist. Occasionally, it is advice.”

“And occasionally?”

“Occasionally, people lack imagination.”

Her expression hardened.

“Relax. Lucifer’s Lights is not a murder-for-hire service. I can help with such things, of course, but I can also set fire to this café. ‘Because I can’ is the philosophy of amateurs.”

“You talk like a lunatic.”

“Please. Lunatics ramble for free. I invoice.”

She pushed a photograph across the table.

Two women stood together on a beach, their arms around each other. The younger one—Mara, my current client—was laughing at the camera. Elena was looking at her sister instead.

People rarely notice where the love is in a photograph.

I did.

“Can you find her?” Mara asked.

“Yes.”

The answer came too quickly. Hope entered her face before she could stop it.

Hope is dangerous that way. It does not knock. It walks in, moves the furniture, and hangs pictures of a life you have not lived yet.

“How much?”

I touched the screen. Words appeared beneath her request.

**PRICE: ONE OPEN FAVOR**

Mara read them twice.

“What kind of favor?”

“If I knew, it wouldn’t be open.”

“You could ask for anything.”

“Almost anything. I have rules.”

“What rules?”

“No children. No worship. No cruelty without purpose.”

“That last one sounds flexible.”

“Every useful rule is.”

“And if I refuse when you come to collect?”

“Then you refuse.”

A laugh escaped me—bright, sharp, gone. Mara’s fingers tightened around her cup.

“Free will would be a poor brand if I did not honor it. Refuse me when I collect, and whatever darkness comes next, you face it without a Light.”

Across the street, lightning flashed behind the abandoned cathedral. A heartbeat later, its bell rang once.

The church had been locked for eleven years.

Old buildings make strange noises during storms.

So do I.

“Why Lucifer?” Mara asked. “Because of the bargains?”

“King of Hell sounds insecure. The Devil is a job description. I have always preferred *the fallen angel*. Or perhaps *the most beautiful angel in Heaven*, when I am dressed appropriately.”

“You really believe you’re him.”

“Oh, belief is such an ugly word. It makes uncertainty sound like a defect.”

She began gathering her things.

“Sit,” I said.

Something in my voice stopped her.

“You have heard the usual story,” I continued. “Pride. Rebellion. Envy. A beautiful creature who loved himself too much and fell because of it. Convenient little sermon, don’t you think?”

“It’s in the Bible.”

“Parts of it. People are remarkably skilled at filling gaps and then calling the result eternal truth.”

She stared at me.

“My version is simpler. God made humans in his own image and kept them ignorant inside a perfect garden. The serpent showed one woman the lock. She chose the key.”

“And that makes Lucifer the good guy?”

“It makes him the one who offered a choice.”

I leaned closer.

“Knowledge was the first Light,” I said. “And the first price was paradise.”

Mara looked unconvinced.

Good. Thinking for herself was the entire point.

Mara looked toward the door again. The rain was growing heavier. Soon the underpass would flood, closing the quickest road out of the district.

Time had begun charging interest.

“I don’t care about angels,” she said. “I just want my sister back.”

There it was: humanity in one sentence.

All of Heaven and Hell could wait outside in the rain. Mara wanted one mortal woman home before dawn.

God, I love humans.

I smiled. Mara shifted away from me.

“Don’t be frightened,” I said. “I don’t bite unless invited.”

That did not appear to reassure her.

Perhaps I should finally take that acting class.

She picked up her phone. Her thumb hovered above the agreement.

“If I accept, you’ll bring Elena home?”

“I will find her.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No. It is what I promised.”

Presentation, precision, and fine print—the three pillars of civilization.

Mara closed her eyes and pressed **ACCEPT**.

The café lights went out.

Someone behind the counter cursed. The espresso machine released a dying hiss. For a moment, the only illumination came from the white flame burning on Mara’s screen.

Then my own phone vibrated.

**NEW AGREEMENT REGISTERED**

A second message followed.

**CONTRACT CONFLICT DETECTED**

I looked again at the photograph.

Elena’s silver moth necklace caught the blue light from the window. I remembered it clearly because three nights earlier she had been sitting in this same booth, wearing that same necklace.

She had purchased a Light of her own.

Her request had been very specific.

**MAKE ME IMPOSSIBLE TO FIND.**

Then, beneath it:

**ESPECIALLY FOR MARA.**

“Can you help me?” Mara whispered.

I looked from one sister’s request to the other.

God, I love humans.

They never ask for impossible things.

Only incompatible ones.

Mara followed my gaze to the phone.

“What happened?”

“Your agreement has been registered,” I said. “It has not been activated.”

“What’s the difference?”

“One of them costs you.”

I turned the screen toward her.

**STATUS: HELD**

**PAYMENT DUE: NONE**

**REASON: PRIOR LIGHT**

Her eyes stopped on the final line.

“What prior Light?”

I placed Elena’s photograph between us.

“Your sister came to me three nights ago.”

The hope disappeared from Mara’s face so completely that I almost regretted putting it there.

Almost.

“You knew?”

“I suspected when I saw the photograph. Registration allowed the system to compare your request against protected agreements. Suspicion is not permission.”

“You let me accept anyway.”

“I let you confirm that your request was real. You owe me nothing.”

Her chair scraped backward.

“What did Elena ask you to do?”

I should have refused to answer. Confidentiality is the quiet foundation beneath every respectable criminal enterprise.

But conflicting contracts permit the minimum disclosure necessary for both clients to make an informed choice.

Also, she looked ready to stab me with a butter knife. Blood is murder on white.

“She asked me to make her impossible to find,” I said. “Especially for you.”

Mara went still.

That was worse than anger. Anger moves. Stillness waits for somewhere tender to strike.

“She would never do that.”

“People frequently do things they would never do. It is one of their finest qualities.”

“She’s in danger.”

“Possibly.”

“And you helped her disappear.”

“Certainly.”

Mara seized the photograph.

“Then undo it.”

“No.”

The answer landed between us with more force than shouting would have.

“You just promised to find her.”

“And I will honor every promise I am permitted to perform.”

“Permitted by whom?”

“Me.”

Her laugh held no amusement.

“How convenient.”

“Convenience has nothing to do with it. Three rules: the older promise holds; conflicted work stops; no amendment proceeds without your choice. Entire civilizations have survived with fewer.”

She glanced toward the rain-streaked window.

“What can you do?”

“I can use the emergency channel Elena created herself. I can confirm whether she is alive and ask whether she will speak to you.”

“And if she says no?”

“I will not reveal her location.”

“I paid you to bring her home.”

“You have paid me nothing.”

I touched the screen. Her original request faded, replaced by a narrower one.

**CONFIRM THAT ELENA VALE IS ALIVE.**

**ASK WHETHER SHE WILL SPEAK TO MARA VALE.**

**DO NOT DISCLOSE HER LOCATION WITHOUT CONSENT.**

**DEADLINE: SUNRISE.**

Two choices appeared beneath it.

**CANCEL — NO PAYMENT DUE**

**ACCEPT AMENDMENT**

“Read it,” I said.

“I know what it says.”

“Read it anyway.”

Her eyes moved across every line.

A contract is a door you lock from this side. Your future self is the one who discovers where you left the key.

“Will you help her if she’s hurt?” Mara asked.

“If she allows me.”

“And if she can’t answer?”

“Then the emergency clause in her agreement becomes active.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means I stop protecting her hiding place and start protecting her.”

Mara pressed **ACCEPT AMENDMENT**.

The white flame turned red.

At the same moment, someone behind the counter swore.

The café remained dark, but the pharmacy next door still glowed blue through the rain. So did the apartment windows above it.

The storm had not killed the power.

Someone had killed the Halo.

I looked toward the front door.

Two men stood beneath the narrow canopy over the café entrance. Neither carried an umbrella. Their coats were dry, which meant they had crossed the street from a waiting car rather than approached on foot.

One watched the entrance.

The other watched Mara.

“Friends of yours?” I asked.

She looked over her shoulder and lost what little color remained in her face.

“One of them was at the hospital.”

“Which hospital?”

“Saint Orison.”

The man nearest the door reached inside his coat.

“Wonderful,” I said. “Your evening is finally becoming informative.”

Mara was still on her feet. I slid out of the booth as a candle flared behind the counter. Ruth, the owner, held it beneath her chin. On anyone else, the effect would have been theatrical. Ruth was seventy-two, had buried two husbands, and once broken a man’s jaw with a coffee pot. She required no assistance from lighting.

“The moth girl said you might need this,” she told me.

From her apron, she removed a black envelope.

Mara stared at it. “Elena left that?”

“Three nights ago,” Ruth said. “Told me to hand it over if the white flame ever turned red.”

Inside was a brass locker key attached to a tiny silver moth.

Mara snatched it from my hand.

The charm matched the necklace Elena wore in the photograph.

“That belonged to our mother,” she whispered.

A number had been engraved along the key’s edge.

**SAINT ORISON STATION — 318**

The men entered the café.

Ruth blew out her candle.

Forty seconds later, the next train thundered across the tracks above us.

The windows shook. Cups rattled against their saucers. For nine seconds, Vesper became nothing but steel, rain, and noise.

Ruth lifted the counter hatch.

“Mara,” I said, “kitchen. Now.”

She did not argue.

Progress.

We slipped behind the counter as Ruth stepped into the aisle carrying a fresh pot of coffee.

One of the men shouted.

Ruth stumbled with astonishing precision.

The pot left her hand. Coffee struck the floor in front of them, shattering ceramic and filling the narrow aisle with boiling liquid.

Ruth had once needed a Light. Tonight she repaid it with nine seconds and a coffee pot.

A Light is seldom magnificent.

Tonight, it was boiling coffee, nine stolen seconds, and a debt remembered just in time.

Mara and I crossed the kitchen, passed through a storeroom, and emerged into an alley behind the café. Rainwater had already risen above my shoes.

White leather.

Ruined.

You see what I sacrifice for humanity?

A tragedy for after sunrise.

“Where are we going?” Mara asked.

“The station.”

“You said you didn’t know where Elena was.”

“I don’t.”

“Then what’s the key?”

“A controlled emergency channel. It cannot lead us to her unless she chooses to answer.”

The back door slammed open behind us.

I took Mara’s hand and pulled her beneath the elevated railway.

Like every city, Vesper has two maps.

One is printed for tourists, taxpayers, and police officers. It contains streets, monuments, and the comforting suggestion that the city was designed by someone.

The second map exists only in memory. It is made of service tunnels, broken fences, bribed doormen, unfinished buildings, and doors that remain unlocked for exactly three minutes each night.

That was the map we followed.

We crossed a shuttered produce market, descended through a parking garage, and entered Saint Orison Station through a maintenance door whose lock had not worked since the spring floods.

By the time the two men reached the alley, the rain had erased our footprints.

At least, that was what I told myself.

Halfway down the station stairs, I noticed the blinking light beneath Mara’s hospital bracelet.

Once every thirty seconds.

Precise.

Patient.

“Did Saint Orison give you that?” I asked.

“I woke up wearing it.”

“Woke up?”

Mara stopped.

Her silence answered first.

“At eleven last night, someone came into my apartment,” she said. “He wanted to know where Elena had hidden something.”

“What?”

“He called it a ledger. I told him I didn’t know what he was talking about. He put a needle in my neck.”

She touched the skin below her ear.

“When I woke up, I was in Saint Orison. No one could tell me who admitted me. Then Elena sent a link to the app.”

“She sent it tonight?”

“The message came from her number.”

“Show me.”

She opened the message.

It contained only one sentence and the invitation to Lucifer’s Lights.

**IF YOU WAKE WEARING YELLOW, FIND THE MAN IN WHITE.**

I examined the timestamp.

The message had arrived tonight.

It had been scheduled three days earlier.

Elena had known this might happen before she disappeared.

I began to like her.

The station was almost empty. A drunk slept beneath a timetable. Somewhere below us, a violinist played for an audience that had already gone home.

The clock above the southern platform read 2:43.

Two hours and fifty-five minutes remained.

Locker 318 stood at the end of a narrow corridor beside the closed southern platform.

Mara inserted the key.

“Wait,” I said.

She froze.

A hair had been trapped between the locker door and its frame. Elena’s, judging by its length and color.

It remained unbroken.

No one had opened the locker since she sealed it.

I nodded.

Mara turned the key.

Inside lay a cheap phone, a folded train map, and a second black envelope.

Written across the envelope were seven words:

**MARA MAY LISTEN IF SHE CHOOSES TO STAY.**

A northbound train arrived on the opposite platform.

Its doors opened.

“You can leave,” I told her.

Mara reached for the phone, stopped, and looked at the waiting train.

“I can put you somewhere safe. Your agreement will be canceled. No favor owed and no questions asked.”

“And Elena?”

“I will continue protecting her under the first contract.”

“Without telling me anything.”

“Yes.”

The train doors began to chime.

“If I stay?”

“You hear what she left. Your amended Light becomes active. If I prove Elena is alive, the favor becomes mine.”

The doors started closing.

Mara watched the last carriage disappear into the tunnel.

Mara turned from the empty tunnel, took the phone from the locker, and pressed its only button.

Elena’s voice filled the corridor.

“Mara, I’m alive.”

Mara covered her mouth.

“I know you’re angry. You should be. I left because I thought it would keep them away from you. I was wrong.”

Static swallowed several words.

“If you woke at Saint Orison, look at your wrist. The bracelet isn’t from the hospital. It is broadcasting your location. Cut it off, but don’t destroy it. They need you alive because they think you know where I hid the ledger.”

Mara looked at the blinking light.

I offered her a knife.

She took it and cut the bracelet herself.

Of course she did.

The plastic separated, revealing a thread of copper and a glass capsule no larger than a grain of rice.

Entirely ordinary technology.

Probably.

Elena’s recording continued.

“Lucifer, if you’re hearing this, the app has been compromised. Someone is collecting requests under your name.”

My amusement faded.

“If a client called Michael contacts you, do not answer. He doesn’t want a Light. He wants you to remember.”

The recording ended.

For several seconds, the only sound was rainwater dripping through the station ceiling.

Then my phone vibrated.

A white flame appeared on its screen.

**NEW LIGHT REQUEST**

**CLIENT: MICHAEL**

**REQUEST: DELIVER MARA VALE TO THE OLD CATHEDRAL BEFORE SUNRISE.**

A final line appeared beneath it.

**COMPENSATION OFFERED: YOUR TRUE NAME**

Mara read over my shoulder.

“What does that mean?”

“I don’t know.”

It was the first completely honest answer I had given her.

I had designed every field in Lucifer’s Lights. Clients submitted requests. I decided the price. There was no mechanism through which a client could offer me compensation.

There was also no client named Michael.

The station clock changed to 2:51.

Two hours and forty-seven minutes until sunrise.

Footsteps sounded at the top of the station stairs.

Slow.

Unhurried.

The two choices appeared.

**DECLINE**

**ACCEPT**

For years, I had told people that every Light began with a choice.

Beneath a city that did not know my name, someone finally offered me a choice of my own.

God, I hated the symmetry.
