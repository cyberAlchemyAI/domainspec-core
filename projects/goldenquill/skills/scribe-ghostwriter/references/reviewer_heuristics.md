# Reviewer Heuristics, Score Upgrades, Denial Distillation, Capital Stacking

Cross-cutting grantwriter reference for the Scribe seat. CIC-agnostic + CLC-agnostic — applies to every applicant world (food systems, educator, public agency, arts & culture, cooperative, ecosystem intermediary) and every capital flow (public purpose, catalytic change, operating capacity, research, market access, credit, sponsorship, shared assets).

Read this before drafting any narrative section in Phase 2. If a draft triggers a heuristic, fold the upgrade requirement into the section before scoring.

## Reviewer Heuristics (red flags to catch in your own draft)

Reviewers do not score what applicants meant. They score what is on the page. The patterns below are the failure modes that turn 90+ drafts into 70s. Catch them in your own prose before Judge does.

### Intent without process

**Trigger phrases:** "will ensure," "is committed to," "plans to," "intends to," "will develop," "will monitor."

**What reviewers see:** Intent is not evidence. Belief is not a system. A claim that the applicant will do X without describing the operating system that produces X reads as aspiration.

**Upgrade requirements:**
- Named responsible role
- Written process
- Review cadence
- Documentation artifact
- Escalation or corrective-action path
- Board / leadership oversight (where the section is about governance, finance, or compliance)

**Action:** For high-stakes sections (compliance, finance, governance, evaluation, sustainability), do not finalize until process is defined. For lower-stakes sections, mark as a gap so the applicant can add a process detail.

### Vision without systems

**Pattern:** Compelling mission, model, or theory of change without execution proof.

**What reviewers see:** Strong idea; launch risk remains. The reviewer believes the applicant wants to do good work; they do not believe the applicant can.

**Upgrade requirements:** implementation timeline, staffing plan, professional-development calendar, operating policies, budget alignment, governance oversight, evaluation system.

### Innovation without coherence

**Pattern:** Many creative practices listed without identifying what is core, what is adopted from proven practice, and how the parts fit together.

**What reviewers see:** Innovation may fracture focus. Risk that the project tries to do everything and finishes nothing.

**Upgrade requirements:** define the core innovation; name proven practices the model adopts; show alignment across schedule / staffing / curriculum / assessment (or production / processing / market / customer for non-education applicants); identify non-negotiables; show feasibility.

### Inclusion without infrastructure

**Pattern:** Claims to serve diverse, marginalized, or under-resourced populations without showing the service model that supports them.

**What reviewers see:** Good aspiration; insufficient delivery system.

**Upgrade requirements:** specific service strategy (differentiated instruction, tiered intervention, accommodations, language access, accessibility supports); educator / staff training plan; family / member / participant communication protocol; progress monitoring; assistive supports where relevant.

### Data without decision cycle

**Pattern:** Data referenced but no description of how data changes staffing, programming, or resource allocation.

**What reviewers see:** Data is decorative, not managerial. The applicant collects information but does not act on it.

**Upgrade requirements:** metric → baseline → collection method → review cadence → responsible owner → decision triggers → intervention plan → reporting loop. Every metric needs an owner and a cadence; every cadence needs a decision rule.

### Shared governance without decision rights

**Pattern:** Collaboration, shared leadership, or distributed authority language without defining who decides what.

**What reviewers see:** Accountability is diffuse. Risk that no one is responsible when something goes wrong.

**Upgrade requirements:** decision-rights matrix; clear board vs. executive vs. operations boundaries; committee responsibilities; escalation path; conflict resolution; documentation process.

### Community engagement without feedback loop

**Pattern:** Outreach, events, surveys, or communication described without showing how stakeholder input changes decisions.

**What reviewers see:** Engagement may be performative. The applicant talks to the community but does not let the community shape the work.

**Upgrade requirements:** input collection method; specific decision points the input influenced; named stakeholder groups; cadence; feedback-to-action reporting; closing-the-loop communication back to participants.

### Sustainability without financial model

**Pattern:** Claims that the work will continue after the grant without showing revenue, costs, enrollment / membership / customer base, staffing, or contingencies.

**What reviewers see:** Post-grant risk is unresolved. The applicant has not modeled survival.

**Upgrade requirements:** multi-year projections; revenue assumptions; demand / enrollment / customer assumptions; staffing ramp; cash-flow logic; contingency plan; board or governance finance oversight.

### Compliance without articulation

**Pattern:** "We will comply with all applicable laws and regulations" without describing the compliance management system.

**What reviewers see:** Compliance is unproven. The applicant says the right words but cannot show the operating apparatus.

**Upgrade requirements:** compliance calendar; designated compliance owner; legal counsel role; staff training schedule; board review cadence; internal audit process; corrective-action procedure.

### Budget without allowability logic

**Pattern:** Cost lines listed without explaining why they are allowable under the RFA, reasonable, necessary, and non-duplicative.

**What reviewers see:** Budget may be noncompliant or weakly justified. Risk of clawback or de-obligation.

**Upgrade requirements:** allowable-cost category citation; tie to a specific allowable activity in the RFA; reasonableness comparison; non-duplication note (no other grant covers this cost); sustainability after grant; proof-of-expenditure plan.

## Score Upgrade Logic (rubric-level moves)

Most public-sector rubrics and many philanthropy rubrics score against four levels: absent → emerging → effective → exemplary (names vary; the pattern is constant). Most applicant drafts land in "emerging." The work of strong drafting is moving each scored section one level up.

### Absent → Emerging

Move from:
- No evidence
- No plan
- No owner
- No policy

Move to:
- Basic plan exists
- Responsible party named
- Partial documentation
- Early implementation described

### Emerging → Effective

Move from:
- General intent
- Partial system
- Inconsistent review
- Unclear documentation

Move to:
- Documented process
- Defined responsible owner
- Implementation cadence
- Clear evidence source
- Role clarity at staff / board level
- Compliance with requirements
- Evidence of monitoring

### Effective → Exemplary

Move from:
- System exists
- Periodic review
- Adequate compliance
- Reactive improvement

Move to:
- Proactive monitoring
- Frequent review and revision
- Documented feedback loops
- Measurable improvement cycle
- Stakeholder input integrated
- Board / leadership accountability
- Evidence of learning over time
- Benchmarking against peer organizations where relevant

Most rubric scoring loss happens at the emerging → effective transition. The applicant has the substance; the draft does not have the named owner, the cadence, and the documentation artifact. Read every section through that lens.

## Drafting Strategy (voice and shape)

### Default voice

- Clear
- Credible
- Human
- Specific
- Applicant-centered (student, member, customer, beneficiary — never the consultant or the funder)
- Operationally grounded
- Non-hype
- Funder-literate

### Avoid

- Generic innovation language ("transformative," "groundbreaking," "revolutionary")
- Empty equity language (terms used without design consequences)
- Inspirational filler that an AI could have produced from no inputs
- Unsupported superlatives
- Philosophy-heavy responses without implementation
- Deficit framing of beneficiaries / participants / communities
- Blame framing toward incumbent institutions without care
- Language implying guaranteed outcomes
- Compliance claims without evidence

### Strengthen

- Direct answer to the prompt in the first sentence
- Beneficiary need (with source-cited evidence)
- Coherence between vision, model, staffing, budget, evaluation
- Community-informed design with a concrete feedback loop
- Team capacity with role-level evidence
- Implementation plan with a date-bearing timeline
- Evidence with citations
- Measurement with named metric + baseline + cadence + owner
- Risk awareness with mitigations
- Sustainability with a financial model
- Funder-specific vocabulary (pulled from the RFA, not invented)

### Short-form seed grant / quick-app response shape

Most foundation seed grants and quick-apply foundation forms use 200–500 character or 1–3 sentence answers. Compress in this order:

1. Direct answer in the first sentence
2. Need or opportunity
3. Model or approach
4. Readiness or evidence
5. Risk mitigation (only if relevant)
6. Durable impact

### Long-form public grant response shape

Federal and state long-form responses score against a rubric criterion. Structure each subsection in this order:

1. Restate the criterion in the funder's language (signals you read the RFA)
2. Concise claim
3. Evidence supporting the claim
4. Implementation process (how the claim becomes reality)
5. Owner / cadence / documentation
6. Outcome measurement
7. Compliance alignment
8. Sustainability or risk mitigation

This shape works for every CIC and every CLC. The substance changes; the structure does not.

## Denial Feedback Distillation

When an applicant receives reviewer feedback on a declined or low-scoring proposal, the feedback is a rich source of reusable intelligence — but only if distilled correctly. Done wrong, it leaks applicant specifics into reusable knowledge (Law 3 violation). Done right, it strengthens future drafts across all clients.

### What to extract from a reviewer report

- Score-limiting omissions
- Repeated reviewer concerns
- Praised elements (these tell you what to keep)
- Weak evidence classes
- Vague claims that scored low
- Compliance gaps
- Governance gaps
- Finance gaps
- Operations gaps
- Implementation gaps
- Language that sounded persuasive but failed scoring

### What to produce

- A reviewer heuristic (add to this file if novel)
- A red-flag trigger (a phrase or pattern to watch for)
- A missing-evidence requirement (an evidence class to surface earlier)
- A score-upgrade rule (a specific move from one level to the next)
- A future-draft blocker (a pre-flight check)
- A client-action item class (something the applicant must produce next time)

### What NOT to extract

- Applicant name or program name
- Specific project details
- Staff names
- Exact geography
- Specific financial figures
- Reviewer identity
- Proprietary narrative language
- Anything that would let a future reader identify the original application

Generalized output should be reusable across every CIC. If you cannot strip the applicant specifics without losing the lesson, the lesson is org-specific — store it in `orgs/<slug>/lessons/` not here.

### Example generalized output

```
red_flag: The narrative states that leadership will monitor compliance but does
  not define a compliance calendar, responsible owner, training cadence, legal
  counsel role, or board reporting process.
blocker_type: compliance_process_missing
recommended_fix:
  - Name compliance owner.
  - Add calendar / review cadence.
  - Describe board oversight.
  - Identify training and legal review.
  - Define corrective-action procedure.
```

Compare: this is reusable across an Educator school launch, a Public Agency facilities upgrade, a Cooperative member-services grant, and an Ecosystem Intermediary capacity build. Same red flag, same fix pattern, different domain.

## Capital Stacking Patterns

Opportunity-driven sequencing patterns that recur across CICs and CLCs. These are NOT CIC-to-CLC bindings (forbidden per architecture v7.0.8). They are META-level grant-sequencing observations the Scribe seat uses to frame later applications in light of earlier wins or losses.

### Planning → Launch

When an award supports planning, technical assistance, facility readiness, governance development, staff training, or compliance setup, the next application can credibly claim:

- Improved readiness
- Reduced launch risk
- External validation (a funder bet on the applicant)
- Implementation infrastructure
- Non-duplicative leverage

The discipline: planning grants are earned credibility for the next round. Frame the next application as building on demonstrated readiness, not as starting from scratch.

### Public award → Philanthropy

When a public award already exists, philanthropic applications can use it as:

- Credibility signal (a federal or state agency vetted the applicant)
- Public-purpose validation
- Readiness proof
- Leverage to attract philanthropic acceleration money

Caution:
- Do not overstate the public funder's endorsement — they funded the work, not the org
- Do not double-count costs across grants
- Explain how philanthropic dollars are complementary, not duplicative

### Philanthropy → Public award

When philanthropic support already exists, public applications can use it as:

- Community validation
- Planning capacity proof
- Match or leverage where the RFA allows
- Early implementation evidence (philanthropy already bet on the model)

Caution:
- Verify match allowability under the RFA
- Avoid substituting philanthropic dollars for required public-sector compliance work

### Denial → Resubmission

When a prior application was declined and reviewer feedback exists, convert it to:

- A gap register (what evidence was missing)
- An evidence checklist (what to gather before resubmitting)
- A narrative revision plan (what prose to rewrite)
- A systems-build plan (what operating systems to add)
- A reviewer-response map (how each prior concern is now addressed)

Narrative framing for the resubmission:

- "Lessons learned"
- "Strengthened operational systems"
- "New oversight and compliance structures"
- "Improved launch readiness"
- "Expanded evidence base"

Reviewers generally reward visible learning from prior feedback. Hiding the prior denial or pretending the application is brand-new is worse than naming the prior cycle and showing what changed.
