# Learning Pack

## Learning objective

After this sequence, a reader should be able to distinguish alternative views from explanatory levels, decide when residue warrants a climb, and explain why formal resemblance is not cognitive evidence.

## Five-minute path

1. Read the research target and safe claim in [`README.md`](README.md).
2. Inspect the construction in [`TOWER.md`](TOWER.md).
3. Read the non-equivalences in [`notation-bridge.md`](notation-bridge.md).
4. Try the DCB calibration case in [`examples/round-trip-comprehension-example.md`](examples/round-trip-comprehension-example.md).
5. Check your answer against the claim ceiling in [`claims/claim-ledger.md`](claims/claim-ledger.md).

## Core lesson

Suppose one idea is shown as prose, a diagram, a contract, and a dashboard. Those are four windows onto the same floor. They become a tower only if a mismatch cannot be named from that floor and a new floor adds the missing vocabulary while preserving what was already known.

Even then, the tower is a design for explanation. To learn whether it helps a person, ask the person to climb, return, and reconstruct the concrete case under fixed scoring rules.

## Self-check

1. Why is a dashboard not automatically above plain language?
2. What kind of residue justifies a new layer?
3. What must be preserved on the return to the concrete case?
4. What does a successful formal tower prove—and what does it not prove?
5. What can an agent structural pretest establish?

## Answers

1. They are audience-specific views of the same bounded claim unless one adds vocabulary required to name previously unresolved residue.
2. Missing-vocabulary or missing-schema residue that survives local repair.
3. Relevant distinctions, evidential ceiling, interpretation limits, and authority boundary.
4. It proves properties of its formal construction, not a human learning mechanism or instrument effect.
5. It can expose ambiguity, leaks, and structural stalls; it cannot establish human comprehension.

## Teach-back prompt

Explain the lane using an ordinary map: several map styles are horizontal views; adding a new legend category is a vertical vocabulary change; successful comprehension means reaching the place and then explaining the route without inventing roads or permissions.
